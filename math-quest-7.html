Integers

Whole numbers, their negatives, and zero

Know: positive and negative numbers
Use: all four operations correctly
Understand: important properties

1. What is an integer?

An integer is a whole number with no fraction or decimal part. Integers include positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero.

…, −4, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, …

Positive integers

Numbers above zero: 1, 2, 3, … Think of money earned or floors above ground.

Negative integers

Numbers below zero: −1, −2, −3, … Think of money owed or temperature below 0°C.

Zero

Zero is neither positive nor negative. It is the centre of the number line.

Opposites

Numbers equally far from zero but on different sides are opposites: 7 and −7.

Try it: walk on the number line

Move the slider. Numbers farther right are always greater.

−3 is less than 0

2. Adding and subtracting integers

Same signs: add

Add the sizes and keep the sign.

−6 + (−4) = −10

Different signs: subtract

Subtract the smaller size from the larger size. Keep the sign of the number with the larger size.

−9 + 5 = −4

Subtract means “add the opposite”

Change subtraction to addition, then change the sign of the next number.

7 − (−3) = 7 + 3 = 10

Absolute value

|−8| means distance from zero, so |−8| = 8. Distance is never negative.

|−8| = 8
Worked example: Find −15 − (−8) + 3.
  1. Change “subtract −8” into “add 8”: −15 + 8 + 3.
  2. −15 + 8 = −7.
  3. −7 + 3 = −4.

3. Multiplying and dividing integers

First multiply or divide the number parts. Then decide the sign using this one rule:

Same signs → positive    |    Different signs → negative

Same signs

(−6) × (−5) = 30
−42 ÷ (−7) = 6

Different signs

(−6) × 5 = −30
42 ÷ (−7) = −6

Sign shortcut: In a product, an even number of negative signs gives a positive answer. An odd number gives a negative answer.

4. Properties you should know

Closure

Adding, subtracting, or multiplying two integers always gives an integer. Division may not: 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5.

Commutative

Order may swap for + and ×.
3 + 5 = 5 + 3; 3 × 5 = 5 × 3.
Not true for − or ÷.

Associative

Grouping may change for + and ×.
(2 + 3) + 4 = 2 + (3 + 4).
Not true for − or ÷.

Identity and distributive

a + 0 = a; a × 1 = a.
a × (b + c) = a×b + a×c.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Integers

Olympiad problems rarely tell you the operation directly. Your job is to turn the story into mathematics, notice a pattern, and check whether the answer makes sense.

Underline facts and the exact question. Choose a variable or draw a small table. Look for pairs, patterns, or a constant change. Calculate, then test the result in the story.

Example 1: A scoring puzzle

In a contest, a correct answer earns 7 points and a wrong answer loses 3 points. Meera answers all 20 questions and scores 100 points. How many answers are correct?

  1. Start with an easier imaginary case: if all 20 answers were correct, the score would be 20 × 7 = 140.
  2. Changing one answer from correct to wrong removes 7 points and then loses 3 more. So each such change lowers the score by 10.
  3. Meera's score is 140 − 100 = 40 points lower. Therefore 40 ÷ 10 = 4 answers were wrong.
  4. Correct answers = 20 − 4 = 16. Check: 16 × 7 − 4 × 3 = 112 − 12 = 100.

Example 2: Pair before you calculate

Find 1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + ··· + 99 − 100 without adding one term at a time.

  1. Group neighbouring terms: (1 − 2) + (3 − 4) + ··· + (99 − 100).
  2. Every pair equals −1.
  3. There are 100 ÷ 2 = 50 pairs, so the sum is 50 × (−1) = −50.
  4. This method is powerful because it replaces 100 calculations with one pattern.

Fractions and Decimals

Different ways to show parts of a whole

Connect: fractions and decimals
Calculate: using four operations
Solve: everyday word problems

1. Fractions are equal parts

In 35, the bottom number (5) says the whole is cut into 5 equal parts. The top number (3) says we take 3 of those parts.

Proper fraction

Top is smaller than bottom, so its value is below 1.

35

Improper fraction

Top is at least as large as bottom, so its value is 1 or more.

74 = 134

Try it: build a fraction

Choose how many equal pieces the whole has, then how many are shaded.

3/4 = 0.75

2. Equivalent and simplest fractions

Equivalent fractions look different but have the same value. Multiply or divide the top and bottom by the same non-zero number.

23 = 2 × 43 × 4 = 812

To simplify, divide the top and bottom by their greatest common factor (GCF).

Worked example: Simplify 4256.
  1. The GCF of 42 and 56 is 14.
  2. 42 ÷ 14 = 3 and 56 ÷ 14 = 4.
  3. Answer: 34.

3. Operations with fractions

Add or subtract

Make the bottom numbers the same. Then add or subtract only the top numbers.

34 + 16 = 912 + 212 = 1112

Multiply

Multiply top by top and bottom by bottom. Simplify.

35 × 109 = 3045 = 23

Divide

Keep the first fraction, change ÷ to ×, and flip the second fraction.

47 ÷ 23 = 47 × 32 = 67

“Of” means multiply

To find a fraction of an amount, multiply.

38 of 64 = 38 × 64 = 24

4. Decimals and place value

Digits after the decimal point show tenths, hundredths, thousandths, and so on.

4.375 = 4 ones + 3 tenths + 7 hundredths + 5 thousandths

Add or subtract decimals

Write decimal points directly under each other. Add zeroes if useful.

8.40 − 2.75 = 5.65

Multiply decimals

Multiply as whole numbers, then count the total decimal places.

1.2 × 0.35 = 0.420 = 0.42

Divide by 10, 100, 1000

Move the decimal point left by 1, 2, or 3 places.

47.5 ÷ 100 = 0.475

Fraction to decimal

Divide the numerator by the denominator.

78 = 7 ÷ 8 = 0.875
Quick check: Multiplying by a number below 1 should make a positive number smaller. Dividing by a number below 1 should make it larger.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Fractions & Decimals

For a “fraction of what remains” problem, do not add the fractions immediately. The second fraction acts on a new, smaller whole.

Call the original whole 1, or use a helpful total. Track what remains after every step. Use a bar model or work backwards. Check that all parts add to the whole.

Example 1: Work backwards from the remainder

A book has some pages. Arjun reads 13 of it on Monday and 14 of the remaining pages on Tuesday. He has 90 pages left. How many pages are in the book?

  1. After Monday, 23 of the book remains.
  2. On Tuesday he reads 14 of that remainder, so he keeps 34 of it.
  3. Fraction left = 23 × 34 = 12.
  4. If half the book is 90 pages, the whole book has 180 pages.

Example 2: Use a convenient imaginary total

In a class, 25 of the students play chess. Of those who do not play chess, 13 play music. What fraction plays neither?

  1. Choose 15 imaginary students because 15 works nicely with both 5 and 3.
  2. Chess players = 25 × 15 = 6, so 9 do not play chess.
  3. Music players among those 9 = 13 × 9 = 3.
  4. Neither = 9 − 3 = 6 out of 15, or 25.

Rational Numbers

Numbers that can be written as one integer divided by another

Recognise: rational numbers
Place: them on a number line
Compare: and calculate accurately

1. What is a rational number?

A rational number can be written as pq, where p and q are integers and q is not zero.

Fractions are rational

35 and −74 already have the required form.

Integers are rational

Any integer can be put over 1: −6 = −61.

Terminating decimals

0.75 = 75100 = 34, so it is rational.

Repeating decimals

0.333… = 13. A repeating decimal is rational too.

Important: The denominator can never be zero. Division by zero has no defined value.

2. Standard form

A rational number is in standard form when:

  1. The numerator and denominator have no common factor except 1.
  2. The denominator is positive.
Worked example: Put 18−24 in standard form.
  1. Move the negative sign to the top: −1824.
  2. The GCF of 18 and 24 is 6.
  3. Divide both by 6: −34.

Try it: place a rational number

Enter a numerator and a positive denominator. See its decimal value and location from −2 to 2.

−3/2 = −1.5

3. Comparing rational numbers

On a number line, the number farther right is greater. For fractions, use a common denominator or cross multiplication.

Worked example: Compare −56 and −79.
  1. Use denominator 18: −1518 and −1418.
  2. −14 is farther right than −15.
  3. So −56 < −79.
Negative-number trap: Among negative numbers, the one with the bigger distance from zero is actually smaller. For example, −8 < −3.

4. Operations and properties

Add and subtract

Use a common denominator. Keep signs carefully.

−23 + 56 = −4 + 56 = 16

Multiply and divide

Use the same fraction rules. To divide, multiply by the reciprocal.

−35 ÷ 910 = −35 × 109 = −23

Additive inverse

A number plus its opposite is zero.

47 + −47 = 0

Multiplicative inverse

A non-zero number times its reciprocal is 1.

47 × 74 = 1

Rational numbers are closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication. They are also closed under division except division by zero. Addition and multiplication are commutative and associative; subtraction and division are not.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Rational Numbers

Hard rational-number problems often hide a simple structure: equal gaps, a midpoint, an unknown part, or two different-looking expressions that must be equal.

Put every number in a comparable form. Mark signs before doing arithmetic. Use midpoint or equal-gap patterns. Substitute your answer to prove it works.

Example 1: Equal spaces on a number line

The points A, B, C and D are equally spaced. A = −56 and D = 23. Find B and C.

  1. The total distance is 23 − (−56) = 46 + 56 = 32.
  2. Four points make three equal gaps, so each gap is 32 ÷ 3 = 12.
  3. B = −56 + 12 = −13.
  4. C = −13 + 12 = 16.

Example 2: Decode a new operation

For rational numbers, define a ★ b = a + b − ab. Find −12 ★ 23.

  1. A new symbol is not a new mystery: replace it exactly with the rule given.
  2. −12 + 23 − [(−12)(23)]
  3. The product is −13, so subtracting it means adding 13.
  4. −12 + 23 + 13 = −12 + 1 = 12.

Exponents and Powers

Short way to write repeated multiplication

Read: base and exponent correctly
Use: laws of powers in simple forms
Evaluate: powers with care

1. What do base and exponent mean?

In 24, the base is 2 and the exponent is 4. It means 2 is multiplied by itself 4 times: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2. The complete expression is called a power.

Repeated multiplication

34 means 3 × 3 × 3 × 3.

Base

The base is the number that is repeated.

Exponent

The exponent tells how many equal factors there are.

Powers of 10

103 = 1,000 and 105 = 100,000.

43 = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64   |   70 = 1

Try it: explore a power

Choose a base and exponent. Watch repeated multiplication turn into a value.

3⁴ = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81

2. Laws of powers

Multiply same base

Keep the base and add the exponents.

am × an = am+n

Divide same base

Keep the base and subtract the exponents.

am ÷ an = am-n

Power of a power

Multiply the exponents.

(am)n = amn

Power of a product

Every factor gets the same exponent.

(ab)n = anbn
Common rule: 52 means 5 × 5, not 5 × 2.

3. Zero, one and negative bases

Zero exponent

Any non-zero base raised to 0 equals 1.

90 = 1

Base 1

Every positive power of 1 is 1.

1100 = 1

Negative base, even exponent

Negative factors pair up, so the answer is positive.

(−2)4 = 16

Negative base, odd exponent

One negative factor remains, so the answer is negative.

(−2)3 = −8
Brackets matter: (−3)2 = 9, but −32 means −(32) = −9.

4. Worked examples

Worked example: Simplify 23 × 24 ÷ 22.
  1. Same base 2, so combine the exponents.
  2. 3 + 4 − 2 = 5.
  3. So the answer is 25 = 32.
Worked example: Find (32)3.
  1. Use the power-of-a-power rule.
  2. Multiply the exponents: 2 × 3 = 6.
  3. (32)3 = 36 = 729.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Exponents & Powers

Large powers are often clues, not invitations to perform long multiplication. Rewrite numbers with a common base and compare their exponents.

Rewrite every number using the same base when possible. Use exponent laws before evaluating. Look for repeating last-digit patterns. Check signs and brackets separately.

Example 1: Compare without calculating

Which is greater: 84 or 46?

  1. Write both numbers with base 2: 8 = 23 and 4 = 22.
  2. 84 = (23)4 = 212.
  3. 46 = (22)6 = 212.
  4. The powers are equal, without evaluating either large number.

Example 2: Find a last digit

What is the last digit of 325?

  1. The last digits of powers of 3 repeat: 3, 9, 7, 1.
  2. The cycle has length 4.
  3. 25 ÷ 4 leaves remainder 1, so use the first number in the cycle.
  4. The last digit of 325 is 3.

Algebraic Expressions

Numbers and letters written together

Identify: terms, factors and coefficients
Combine: like terms correctly
Form: and simplify simple expressions

1. Terms, factors and coefficients

An algebraic expression has numbers, letters and operation signs. A term is one part separated by + or −.

Term

In 3x + 5, there are 2 terms: 3x and 5.

Coefficient

The number multiplying the letter is the coefficient. In 7a, the coefficient is 7.

Factor

Numbers or letters that are multiplied are factors. In 4xy, the factors are 4, x and y.

Like terms

Only terms with the same letters and powers can be added.

3x, 5y, 2a + 7, 4m − 1

2. Like and unlike terms

Terms are like terms only when their variable parts match exactly. Their coefficients may be different.

Like terms

3x and −8x have the same variable part x.

3x − 8x = −5x

Unlike terms

2x, 2y and 2x2 have different variable parts, so they cannot combine.

Constant terms

Numbers without variables are like terms.

9 − 4 + 2 = 7

Hidden coefficient

x means 1x, and −y means −1y.

x + 4x = 5x

Try it: combine like terms

Choose two coefficients to simplify ax + bx.

x + x = 8x

3. Operations with expressions

Add like terms

3x + 5x = 8x.

3a + 2a = 5a

Subtract carefully

Change the sign of the whole term when it is subtracted.

7m − 2m = 5m

Use distributive property

Multiply the number outside the bracket by every term inside.

4(x + 3) = 4x + 12

Translate words

"5 more than x" means x + 5. "Twice a number" means 2x.

4. Evaluate and form expressions

To evaluate an expression, replace each variable with its given value and follow the order of operations.

Worked example: Evaluate 3x2 − 2x + 4 when x = −2.
  1. Substitute with brackets: 3(−2)2 − 2(−2) + 4.
  2. Do the power first: (−2)2 = 4.
  3. 3 × 4 + 4 + 4 = 20.
Worked example: Simplify 3x + 5x − 2 + 7.
  1. Group like terms: 3x + 5x and −2 + 7.
  2. 3x + 5x = 8x.
  3. −2 + 7 = 5.
  4. Answer: 8x + 5.
Worked example: Expand 2(a + 6) + 3a.
  1. Multiply 2 by both terms inside the bracket: 2a + 12.
  2. Add 3a.
  3. 2a + 3a + 12 = 5a + 12.
Remember: 2x and 2y are not like terms. Only 2x and 5x can join.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Algebraic Expressions

In harder problems, an expression often hides a pattern. Simplify first, or use a known value instead of solving for every variable.

Translate words into symbols one phrase at a time. Remove brackets before combining like terms. Use identities to avoid unnecessary calculation. Substitute the result back as a check.

Example 1: Use the information directly

If a + b = 12, find 3a + 3b − 5.

  1. Factor the matching part: 3a + 3b = 3(a + b).
  2. Use a + b = 12.
  3. 3(a + b) − 5 = 3 × 12 − 5.
  4. The value is 31. We never needed a and b separately.

Example 2: Spot a square pattern

Simplify (x + 3)2 − x2.

  1. Expand (x + 3)2 as x2 + 6x + 9.
  2. Subtract x2.
  3. The x2 terms cancel.
  4. The simplified expression is 6x + 9.

Simple Equations

Find the unknown by keeping both sides equal

Understand: an equation is a balance
Solve: equations and word problems
Check: every solution by substitution

1. Expressions, equations and solutions

An expression, such as 3x + 2, has no equals sign. An equation, such as 3x + 2 = 17, says that two expressions have the same value. A value that makes the statement true is its solution.

Unknown

A letter stands for a number we do not yet know. In x + 5 = 12, x is the unknown.

Equal sides

The equals sign means “has the same value as,” not “the answer comes next.”

Solution

x = 7 solves x + 5 = 12 because 7 + 5 and 12 are equal.

Identity

Some statements, such as 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6, are true for every x.

2. The balance method

Imagine an equation as a balanced scale. To keep it balanced, perform the same operation on both sides. Use an inverse operation to undo what has been done to x.

Undo addition

Subtract the same number from both sides.

x + 8 = 21 → x = 13

Undo subtraction

Add the same number to both sides.

x − 6 = 10 → x = 16

Undo multiplication

Divide both sides by the non-zero multiplier.

5x = 35 → x = 7

Undo division

Multiply both sides by the divisor.

x ÷ 4 = 9 → x = 36

Try it: solve ax + b = c

Choose the three numbers. The solver shows both inverse-operation steps and checks the balance.

3x − 5 = 16 → x = 7

3. Two-step equations and brackets

Undo operations in reverse order. For ax + b = c, remove b first, then divide by a. If brackets occur, either expand them or divide away the outside factor.

ax + b = c  →  ax = c − b  →  x = (c − b) ÷ a
Worked example: Solve 4x − 9 = 23.
  1. Add 9 to both sides: 4x = 32.
  2. Divide both sides by 4: x = 8.
  3. Check: 4 × 8 − 9 = 32 − 9 = 23.
Worked example: Solve 3(x + 2) = 27.
  1. Divide both sides by 3: x + 2 = 9.
  2. Subtract 2 from both sides: x = 7.
  3. Check: 3(7 + 2) = 27.

4. Turning words into equations

Choose a letter, translate one phrase at a time, solve, and answer with the correct unit. Words such as “is” or “gives” often become an equals sign.

More than

“5 more than a number is 18” becomes x + 5 = 18.

Times a number

“Three times a number is 24” becomes 3x = 24.

Consecutive numbers

Consecutive integers can be written x, x + 1, x + 2, …

Always check

Substitute the solution into the original equation, not a partly simplified one.

Worked example: A taxi charges ₹40 plus ₹12 per kilometre. The fare is ₹136. How far did it travel?
  1. Let the distance be d km. Then 40 + 12d = 136.
  2. Subtract 40: 12d = 96.
  3. Divide by 12: d = 8 km.
  4. Check: ₹40 + 12 × ₹8 = ₹136.
Common mistake: “Moving” a term is only a shortcut. The real reason its operation changes is that you perform an inverse operation on both sides.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Simple Equations

Harder equation problems hide the unknown inside a pattern or story. Build one accurate equation before doing arithmetic.

Choose one unknown and state what it means. Translate every condition before solving. Use balance or work backwards. Check the result in every condition.

Example 1: Consecutive integers

Three consecutive integers have sum 72. Find them.

  1. Let the smallest integer be x. The next two are x + 1 and x + 2.
  2. Build the equation: x + (x + 1) + (x + 2) = 72.
  3. So 3x + 3 = 72, then 3x = 69 and x = 23.
  4. The integers are 23, 24 and 25. Their sum is 72.

Example 2: Reverse the machine

A number is doubled, 7 is added, and the result is divided by 3 to give 11. Find the number.

  1. Let the number be x: (2x + 7) ÷ 3 = 11.
  2. Multiply both sides by 3: 2x + 7 = 33.
  3. Subtract 7 and divide by 2: x = 26 ÷ 2 = 13.
  4. Check the machine: 13 × 2 = 26, +7 = 33, ÷3 = 11.

Comparing Quantities

Use ratio, proportion and the unitary method

Compare: quantities using ratios
Recognise: direct proportion
Apply: unitary reasoning to real problems

1. Ratios compare quantities

A ratio compares quantities by division. The quantities must use the same unit before they are compared, and the order of the terms matters.

Simplest form

Divide both terms by their GCF: 18:24 = 3:4.

Order matters

If red:blue = 2:5, then blue:red = 5:2, not 2:5.

Match units

2 m : 50 cm = 200 cm : 50 cm = 4:1.

Part to whole

If boys:girls = 3:5, then boys:class = 3:(3 + 5) = 3:8.

2. Equivalent ratios and proportion

Equivalent ratios are made by multiplying or dividing both terms by the same non-zero number. An equality of two ratios is a proportion.

a:b = c:d   means   a × d = b × c

Scale factor

4:7 = 12:21 because both terms were multiplied by 3.

Cross products

3:5 and 12:20 are proportional because 3 × 20 = 5 × 12.

Missing term

5:8 = x:40 gives 8x = 200, so x = 25.

Direct proportion

When one quantity is multiplied by k, the other is also multiplied by k.

Try it: scale a ratio

Enter a base ratio a:b and a new first quantity. The matching second quantity keeps the ratio unchanged.

3:5 = 12:20

3. The unitary method

The unitary method first finds the value for one unit, then multiplies to find the required number of units. Write the unit beside every answer.

Worked example: If 8 notebooks cost ₹120, what do 15 notebooks cost?
  1. Cost of 1 notebook = ₹120 ÷ 8 = ₹15.
  2. Cost of 15 notebooks = 15 × ₹15.
  3. The cost is ₹225.
Worked example: A car travels 156 km in 3 hours at constant speed. How far in 5 hours?
  1. Distance in 1 hour = 156 ÷ 3 = 52 km.
  2. Distance in 5 hours = 52 × 5.
  3. The car travels 260 km.

4. Sharing and rates

Divide in a ratio

Add the ratio parts, find one part, then multiply. ₹420 in 3:4 gives ₹180 and ₹240.

Unit rate

A rate compares different units. 180 km in 3 h is 60 km/h.

Best buy

Compare cost per item or per kilogram, not just the packet prices.

Not always direct

More workers may take less time. That is inverse, not direct, proportion.

Reasonableness check: If twice as many identical items are bought, a directly proportional cost should also double.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Comparing Quantities

Ratio problems become easier when every quantity is written as a multiple of one common “part.”

Make units and ratio order match. Represent terms as multiples of one part. Use totals or differences to find one part. Check the final ratio and total.

Example 1: Use the difference

Two numbers are in the ratio 5:8, and their difference is 39. Find the numbers.

  1. Write the numbers as 5k and 8k.
  2. The difference is 8k − 5k = 3k.
  3. So 3k = 39 and k = 13.
  4. The numbers are 65 and 104; their difference is 39.

Example 2: A changed ratio

Red and blue beads are in the ratio 3:5. After 12 red beads are added, the ratio becomes 1:1. How many blue beads were there?

  1. Let red = 3k and blue = 5k.
  2. After adding red beads, equal numbers mean 3k + 12 = 5k.
  3. Thus 2k = 12, so k = 6.
  4. Blue beads = 5 × 6 = 30. Red becomes 18 + 12 = 30.

Percentage and Its Applications

Use “out of 100” to solve everyday problems

Convert: fractions, decimals and percents
Calculate: increase, decrease and discount
Apply: profit, loss and simple interest

1. Percent means per hundred

A percentage is a fraction whose denominator is 100. This makes it a convenient way to compare parts of different-sized wholes.

Percent to fraction

35% = 35/100 = 7/20. Always simplify the fraction.

Fraction to percent

Multiply by 100%: 3/5 × 100% = 60%.

Percent to decimal

Divide by 100: 7.5% = 0.075.

Decimal to percent

Multiply by 100: 0.42 = 42%.

Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100%

2. Finding a part, whole or rate

Find the part

Part = percent × whole.

15% of 240 = 0.15 × 240 = 36

Find the whole

Whole = part ÷ decimal rate.

30 is 20% of 150

Find the rate

Rate = part ÷ whole × 100%.

18 out of 24 = 75%

Mental benchmarks

10% is one tenth, 5% is half of 10%, and 1% is one hundredth.

Try it: percentage calculator

Find a percentage of an amount, or apply a percentage increase or decrease.

% 15% of 240 = 36

3. Increase, decrease and discount

Percentage change is always compared with the original value. A discount is a percentage decrease from the marked price.

New value = original × (1 ± rate/100)
Worked example: A ₹1,200 bicycle helmet is discounted by 15%. Find the sale price.
  1. Discount = 15% of ₹1,200 = ₹180.
  2. Sale price = ₹1,200 − ₹180.
  3. The sale price is ₹1,020.
Worked example: A town's population rises from 8,000 to 8,600. Find the percentage increase.
  1. Increase = 8,600 − 8,000 = 600.
  2. Compare with the original: 600 ÷ 8,000 × 100.
  3. Percentage increase = 7.5%.

4. Profit, loss and simple interest

Profit

Profit = SP − CP. Profit% = profit ÷ CP × 100.

Loss

Loss = CP − SP. Loss% = loss ÷ CP × 100.

Simple interest

SI = PRT/100, where P is principal, R is annual rate, and T is time in years.

Amount

The final amount is principal plus interest: A = P + SI.

Worked example: Find the amount on ₹2,500 at 6% simple interest for 3 years.
  1. SI = 2,500 × 6 × 3 ÷ 100 = ₹450.
  2. Amount = ₹2,500 + ₹450.
  3. The amount is ₹2,950.
Watch the base: Profit and loss percentages use cost price, while discount percentage uses marked price.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Percentages

Successive percentage changes act on different bases. Use multipliers rather than simply adding or subtracting the rates.

Identify the original reference value. Turn each percent into a multiplier. Apply changes one at a time. Check whether the result should rise or fall.

Example 1: Equal rates do not cancel

A price increases by 20% and then decreases by 20%. What is the overall change?

  1. Use an imaginary original price of ₹100.
  2. After a 20% increase, the price is ₹120.
  3. The 20% decrease is now 20% of ₹120 = ₹24, leaving ₹96.
  4. Compared with ₹100, this is an overall 4% decrease.

Example 2: Work backwards through a discount

After a 15% discount, a jacket costs ₹1,530. What was its marked price?

  1. After the discount, 100% − 15% = 85% of the marked price remains.
  2. So 85% corresponds to ₹1,530.
  3. Marked price = 1,530 ÷ 0.85 = ₹1,800.
  4. Check: 15% of ₹1,800 is ₹270; ₹1,800 − ₹270 = ₹1,530.

Lines and Angles

Use angle relationships to find unknown measures

Name: lines, rays and angle pairs
Use: intersecting-line rules
Reason: with parallel lines and a transversal

1. Lines and types of angles

A line continues forever in both directions, a ray has one endpoint, and a line segment has two endpoints. An angle measures the turn between two rays with a common endpoint.

Acute angle

Greater than 0° but less than 90°.

Right angle

Exactly 90°, often marked with a small square.

Obtuse angle

Greater than 90° but less than 180°.

Straight and reflex

A straight angle is 180°; a reflex angle is between 180° and 360°.

2. Angle pairs and intersecting lines

Complementary

Two angles whose measures add to 90°.

Supplementary

Two angles whose measures add to 180°.

Linear pair

Adjacent angles on a straight line; they are supplementary.

Vertically opposite

Opposite angles formed by two intersecting lines; they are equal.

Around a point: 360°   |   On a straight line: 180°

Try it: related-angle explorer

Choose an angle and find either its complement or supplement.

° 90° − 37° = 53°

3. Parallel lines and a transversal

A transversal crosses two or more lines. When the crossed lines are parallel, predictable angle relationships appear.

Corresponding angles

Same relative corner at each crossing; they are equal.

Alternate interior angles

Inside the parallel lines on opposite sides of the transversal; they are equal.

Co-interior angles

Inside on the same side of the transversal; they add to 180°.

Alternate exterior angles

Outside on opposite sides of the transversal; they are equal.

Worked example: A transversal crosses parallel lines. One obtuse angle is 118°. Find an adjacent acute angle.
  1. The two angles form a linear pair, so they add to 180°.
  2. 180° − 118° = 62°.
  3. The adjacent angle is 62°. All acute angles in the diagram are also 62°.

4. Solving angle equations

Replace the geometric relationship with an equation, solve it, and then find the requested angle. State the angle rule that justifies the equation.

Worked example: Vertically opposite angles are (3x + 8)° and (5x − 20)°. Find x and the angle.
  1. Vertically opposite angles are equal: 3x + 8 = 5x − 20.
  2. Add 20 and subtract 3x: 28 = 2x, so x = 14.
  3. The angle is 3 × 14 + 8 = 50°.
Worked example: Two co-interior angles are (2x + 10)° and (3x − 5)°. Find x.
  1. Co-interior angles sum to 180°.
  2. 2x + 10 + 3x − 5 = 180, so 5x + 5 = 180.
  3. 5x = 175 and x = 35.
Good geometry habit: Never trust how large an angle looks. Mark equal angles and write the rule before calculating.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Lines & Angles

Angle puzzles are chains of facts. Mark only what a rule proves, then carry that value to the next relationship.

Label unknown angles clearly. Name the rule used at each step. Build equations from 90°, 180° or 360°. Check all angles are in a sensible range.

Example 1: Angles around a point

Four angles around a point are x°, 2x°, 3x° and 4x°. Find the largest angle.

  1. Angles around a point add to 360°.
  2. x + 2x + 3x + 4x = 10x = 360°.
  3. Therefore x = 36°.
  4. The largest angle is 4x = 4 × 36° = 144°.

Example 2: Bisected linear pair

Two adjacent angles form a straight line. The larger is twice the smaller. The larger angle is bisected. Find each of the three resulting angles.

  1. Let the smaller original angle be x; the larger is 2x.
  2. They form a straight line, so x + 2x = 180° and x = 60°.
  3. The larger angle is 120°. Bisecting it makes two angles of 60°.
  4. The three resulting angles are 60°, 60° and 60°.

The Triangle and Its Properties

Connect sides, angles and special line segments

Classify: triangles by sides and angles
Use: angle and side relationships
Apply: the Pythagoras theorem

1. Classifying triangles

A triangle has three sides, three vertices and three interior angles. It can be classified by its side lengths or by its angles.

Scalene

All three sides have different lengths, so all three angles are different.

Isosceles

Two equal sides have equal opposite angles.

Equilateral

All sides are equal and every angle is 60°.

By angles

A triangle is acute, right or obtuse according to its largest angle.

2. Interior and exterior angle rules

Interior angles: A + B + C = 180°
Exterior angle = sum of the two remote interior angles
Worked example: An isosceles triangle has a vertex angle of 44°. Find each base angle.
  1. The two base angles are equal.
  2. Their total is 180° − 44° = 136°.
  3. Each base angle is 136° ÷ 2 = 68°.
Worked example: An exterior angle is 125° and one remote interior angle is 48°. Find the other.
  1. Exterior angle = sum of the remote interior angles.
  2. 125° = 48° + x°.
  3. x = 125° − 48° = 77°.

Try it: triangle angle and right-triangle explorer

Find a third angle, or switch modes to find a hypotenuse from two perpendicular legs.

Angle C = 180° − 48° − 61° = 71°

3. Medians, altitudes and side rules

Median

A segment from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.

Altitude

A perpendicular segment from a vertex to the line containing the opposite side.

Triangle inequality

The sum of any two side lengths must be greater than the third side.

Side–angle link

The longest side lies opposite the largest angle, and equal sides lie opposite equal angles.

Worked example: Can 4 cm, 7 cm and 12 cm form a triangle?
  1. Test the two shorter sides: 4 + 7 = 11.
  2. Since 11 is not greater than 12, the sides cannot close.
  3. They do not form a triangle.

4. Right triangles and Pythagoras

In a right triangle, the side opposite the 90° angle is the hypotenuse. It is always the longest side.

leg² + leg² = hypotenuse²   or   a² + b² = c²
Worked example: A right triangle has hypotenuse 13 cm and one leg 5 cm. Find the other leg.
  1. Let the missing leg be b: 5² + b² = 13².
  2. b² = 169 − 25 = 144.
  3. b = √144 = 12 cm.
Important: The Pythagoras theorem applies only to right triangles, and c must be the hypotenuse.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Triangle Properties

Advanced triangle questions combine several basic facts. Add useful labels and decide which rule connects the known information to the unknown.

Mark equal sides, equal angles and right angles. Use the 180° sum before guessing. Test side lengths with triangle inequality. Check whether Pythagoras is allowed.

Example 1: Algebra in an isosceles triangle

The vertex angle of an isosceles triangle is (2x + 10)°. Each base angle is (3x − 5)°. Find all angles.

  1. The interior angles total 180°: (2x + 10) + 2(3x − 5) = 180.
  2. Simplify: 2x + 10 + 6x − 10 = 180, so 8x = 180 and x = 22.5.
  3. Vertex angle = 2(22.5) + 10 = 55°; each base angle = 3(22.5) − 5 = 62.5°.
  4. The angles are 55°, 62.5° and 62.5°, and they sum to 180°.

Example 2: Shortest integer side

Two sides of a triangle are 7 cm and 12 cm. What is the smallest possible whole-number length of the third side?

  1. If the third side is x, triangle inequality gives 7 + x > 12.
  2. Therefore x > 5. Equality would make a straight, flat shape, not a triangle.
  3. The other inequalities are automatically true for a small positive x here.
  4. The smallest whole number greater than 5 is 6 cm.

Congruence of Triangles

Prove when triangles have exactly the same shape and size

Understand: congruence and correspondence
Choose: SSS, SAS, ASA or RHS
Prove: equal parts using CPCT

1. Congruence and correspondence

Two triangles are congruent when one can fit exactly on the other after sliding, turning or flipping. Their corresponding sides and angles are equal; their position does not matter.

Matching order

△ABC ≅ △PQR means A ↔ P, B ↔ Q and C ↔ R.

Corresponding sides

From that order, AB = PQ, BC = QR and AC = PR.

Corresponding angles

∠A = ∠P, ∠B = ∠Q and ∠C = ∠R.

CPCT

After congruence is proved, Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are equal.

Worked example: If △ABC ≅ △DCE, find the side matching AC and the angle matching ∠B.
  1. Read the letters in order: A ↔ D, B ↔ C and C ↔ E.
  2. Therefore AC ↔ DE and ∠B ↔ ∠C.
  3. So AC = DE and ∠B = ∠DCE.

2. The four congruence tests

SSS

All three pairs of corresponding sides are equal.

SAS

Two side pairs and the included angle between them are equal.

ASA

Two angle pairs and the included side are equal; the third angle is then fixed.

RHS

For right triangles: equal hypotenuses and one equal corresponding leg.

Not valid: AAA fixes shape but not size, and SSA can sometimes make two different triangles.

Try it: congruence-test classifier

Select the information known to be equal in two triangles. The classifier identifies a guaranteed test, if one applies.

SSS guarantees congruence.

3. Choosing and applying a test

Mark the information supplied, check that parts correspond, and use the shortest valid test. Do not use CPCT until after congruence has been established.

Worked example: AB = DE, BC = EF and ∠B = ∠E. Prove △ABC ≅ △DEF.
  1. ∠B lies between AB and BC; ∠E lies between DE and EF.
  2. Two corresponding sides and their included angles are equal.
  3. Therefore △ABC ≅ △DEF by SAS.
Worked example: Right triangles PQR and XYZ have ∠Q = ∠Y = 90°, PR = XZ and PQ = XY.
  1. PR and XZ are opposite the right angles, so they are the hypotenuses.
  2. The hypotenuses and one corresponding leg are equal.
  3. Therefore △PQR ≅ △XYZ by RHS.

4. Isosceles triangles and shared parts

A common side equals itself (the reflexive fact), which often supplies a missing pair. In an isosceles triangle, drawing a segment from the vertex to the base can create congruent triangles.

Worked example: In isosceles △ABC, AB = AC. D is the midpoint of BC. Show that ∠BAD = ∠DAC.
  1. AB = AC (given), BD = DC (midpoint), and AD = AD (common side).
  2. Thus △ABD ≅ △ACD by SSS.
  3. By CPCT, ∠BAD = ∠DAC; AD bisects the vertex angle.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Congruence

Congruence proofs become easier when you search for hidden equalities: common sides, radii of one circle, midpoint halves and vertically opposite angles.

Write the correspondence before the test. Mark given and hidden equal parts. Use one valid test to prove congruence. Use CPCT only after the proof.

Example 1: Diagonals that bisect each other

Diagonals AC and BD of quadrilateral ABCD meet at O with AO = OC and BO = OD. Prove AB = CD.

  1. Consider △AOB and △COD.
  2. AO = OC and BO = OD are given.
  3. ∠AOB = ∠COD because vertically opposite angles are equal.
  4. Thus the triangles are congruent by SAS, so AB = CD by CPCT.

Example 2: Perpendicular bisector reasoning

M is the midpoint of AB and PM ⟂ AB. Prove PA = PB.

  1. Compare right triangles △PMA and △PMB.
  2. MA = MB because M is the midpoint, and PM is common.
  3. The included angles ∠PMA and ∠PMB are both 90°.
  4. The triangles are congruent by SAS, so PA = PB by CPCT.

Perimeter and Area

Measure boundaries, surfaces and composite shapes

Distinguish: perimeter from area
Calculate: common plane shapes
Reason: with units and composite figures

1. Perimeter: distance around

Perimeter is the total length of a closed boundary. Include every outside edge once and use linear units such as cm or m.

Square

P = 4a, where a is the side length.

Rectangle

P = 2(l + b), not l × b.

Regular polygon

P = number of sides × one side.

Circle

C = 2πr = πd; circumference is the circle's perimeter.

Worked example: A rectangular garden is 18 m by 11 m. How much fencing is needed for one complete boundary?
  1. P = 2(l + b) = 2(18 + 11).
  2. P = 2 × 29 = 58 m.
  3. Fencing is a length, so square units are not used.

2. Area: surface covered

Area counts unit squares covering a region, so it uses square units. A perpendicular height—not a sloping side—is required for triangles and parallelograms.

Rectangle and square

A = l × b; for a square, A = a².

Triangle

A = ½bh because it is half a matching parallelogram.

Parallelogram

A = bh, using perpendicular height.

Circle

A = πr². Squaring the diameter instead gives an area four times too large.

1 m² = 10,000 cm²   because   1 m = 100 cm

Try it: perimeter and area calculator

Select a shape. For a triangle, enter base, height and the other two sides; for a circle only the first value (radius) is used.

P = 40 units; A = 96 square units

3. Composite shapes and missing dimensions

Split a composite figure into familiar non-overlapping pieces. Add their areas, or subtract a cut-out. For perimeter, trace only the exposed outside boundary.

Worked example: A 10 cm × 8 cm rectangle has a 4 cm × 3 cm corner cut out. Find the remaining area.
  1. Whole area = 10 × 8 = 80 cm².
  2. Cut-out area = 4 × 3 = 12 cm².
  3. Remaining area = 80 − 12 = 68 cm².
Worked example: A rectangle has area 96 cm² and length 12 cm. Find its breadth and perimeter.
  1. b = A ÷ l = 96 ÷ 12 = 8 cm.
  2. P = 2(12 + 8) = 40 cm.
  3. The breadth is 8 cm and perimeter is 40 cm.

4. Circles and practical measurement

The radius joins the centre to the circle; the diameter is twice the radius. Use π = 22/7 when the radius is a multiple of 7 unless another approximation is given.

Radius and diameter

d = 2r and r = d ÷ 2.

Circumference

One turn of a wheel travels πd.

Semicircle area

½πr²; halve the area of the full circle.

Semicircle perimeter

πr + 2r; include the straight diameter.

Unit check: Convert all lengths to one unit before using a formula. Perimeter is linear; area is squared.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Perimeter & Area

Olympiad mensuration rewards seeing invariant lengths, rearranging pieces and choosing a convenient whole-minus-gap calculation.

Sketch and label every known length. Convert units before calculating. Split, rearrange or subtract regions. Check size and units at the end.

Example 1: Same perimeter, different area

A rectangle with whole-number sides has perimeter 24 cm. Which dimensions give the greatest area?

  1. 2(l + b) = 24, so l + b = 12.
  2. Possible pairs include 1 and 11, 2 and 10, …, 6 and 6.
  3. Their products grow as the sides become closer: 11, 20, 27, 32, 35, 36.
  4. The greatest area is 36 cm², from a 6 cm square.

Example 2: Path around a garden

A 12 m × 8 m garden has a 1 m wide path outside it. Find the path's area.

  1. The path adds 1 m on both sides of each dimension.
  2. Outer dimensions are 14 m × 10 m, so outer area is 140 m².
  3. Garden area is 12 × 8 = 96 m².
  4. Path area = 140 − 96 = 44 m².

Data Handling

Organise information, compare centre and chance

Summarise: mean, median, mode and range
Represent: tables, bars and circle graphs
Predict: probability from outcomes

1. Measures of centre and spread

A data set can be described by a typical value and by how spread out it is. Always order the values before finding the median.

Mean

Add all values and divide by their number. Mean = sum ÷ count.

Median

Middle ordered value; for an even count, average the two middle values.

Mode

Most frequent value. Data can have no mode or more than one mode.

Range

Largest − smallest; it measures spread, not centre.

Worked example: For 4, 9, 7, 7, 13, 8, find all four measures.
  1. Order: 4, 7, 7, 8, 9, 13; sum = 48 and count = 6.
  2. Mean = 48 ÷ 6 = 8; median = (7 + 8) ÷ 2 = 7.5.
  3. Mode = 7 and range = 13 − 4 = 9.

Try it: data statistics calculator

Enter 2–12 finite numbers separated by commas. Repeated modes are reported clearly.

Mean 8; median 7; mode 7; range 9

2. Frequency tables and graphs

A frequency records how often a value occurs. Graph scales must use equal steps, and every graph needs a title and labelled axes.

Bar graph

Separate equal-width bars compare categories.

Double bar graph

Paired bars compare two related groups using one scale.

Circle graph

A sector angle is category fraction × 360°.

Misleading scale

A cut-off or uneven axis can exaggerate differences; read labels before judging.

Worked example: In a survey of 40 pupils, 14 choose football. Find the football sector angle in a circle graph.
  1. Football fraction = 14/40 = 7/20.
  2. Angle = 7/20 × 360°.
  3. The sector angle is 126°.

3. Experimental and theoretical probability

For equally likely outcomes, theoretical probability compares favourable outcomes with all possible outcomes. Experimental probability uses actual trial results.

P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total equally likely outcomes

Probability scale

0 means impossible, 1 means certain, and all probabilities lie between.

Complement

P(not E) = 1 − P(E).

Experiment

Relative frequency = event occurrences ÷ number of trials.

More trials

Experimental probability usually gets closer to theoretical probability.

Worked example: A bag has 5 red, 3 blue and 2 green counters. Find P(not blue).
  1. Total counters = 10 and non-blue counters = 5 + 2 = 7.
  2. P(not blue) = 7/10.
  3. Check by complement: 1 − P(blue) = 1 − 3/10 = 7/10.

4. Choosing a useful average

The mean uses every value but is pulled by an extreme value. The median is often more representative for house prices or incomes containing an outlier, while the mode identifies the most popular category.

Reason before calculating: Ask whether the data are numerical, whether an outlier exists, and what “typical” should mean in context.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Data Handling

Hard data questions often hide a missing value or ask how one change affects several measures. Work with the total behind the mean.

Order data and record the count. Recover totals from mean × count. Track only values changed or added. Check probabilities lie from 0 to 1.

Example 1: Correcting a recorded value

The mean of 8 values was calculated as 15. Later 18 was found to have been recorded as 12. Find the correct mean.

  1. The incorrect total was 8 × 15 = 120.
  2. Replace 12 by 18, increasing the total by 6.
  3. Correct total = 126.
  4. Correct mean = 126 ÷ 8 = 15.75.

Example 2: Two dice and a sum

Two fair six-sided dice are rolled. Find the probability that their sum is 8.

  1. There are 6 × 6 = 36 ordered, equally likely outcomes.
  2. Favourable pairs are (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3) and (6,2).
  3. There are 5 favourable outcomes.
  4. The probability is 5/36.

Symmetry and Visualising Solid Shapes

Connect transformations, nets and three-dimensional views

Identify: line and rotational symmetry
Describe: faces, edges and vertices
Visualise: nets, slices and 2D views

1. Line symmetry

A line of symmetry divides a figure into mirror-image halves. If folded along that line, every point on one half lands on its matching point on the other.

Square

4 lines: two through opposite sides and two diagonals.

Rectangle

2 lines through the midpoints of opposite sides; diagonals are not symmetry lines.

Equilateral triangle

3 lines, each from a vertex to the opposite side's midpoint.

Circle

Every diameter is a symmetry line, so there are infinitely many.

Worked example: How many lines of symmetry does a regular hexagon have?
  1. Three lines join opposite vertices.
  2. Three more join midpoints of opposite sides.
  3. Total = 6 lines of symmetry.

2. Rotational symmetry

The order of rotational symmetry is the number of times a shape matches itself during one full 360° turn, including the finishing position.

Smallest angle of rotation = 360° ÷ order

Square

Order 4; it matches every 90°.

Non-square rectangle

Order 2; smallest matching turn is 180°.

Regular n-gon

Rotational order n and smallest angle 360°/n.

Line versus rotation

A shape can have rotational symmetry but no line symmetry, as a general parallelogram does.

3. Solids and their properties

A face is a flat or curved surface, an edge is where surfaces meet, and a vertex is a corner. For convex polyhedra, Euler's relation checks the count.

Faces + Vertices = Edges + 2

Cube or cuboid

6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices.

Triangular prism

5 faces, 9 edges and 6 vertices.

Square pyramid

5 faces, 8 edges and 5 vertices.

Cylinder and cone

A cylinder has two circular flat faces; a cone has one circular flat face and one vertex.

Try it: symmetry and solid-properties explorer

Choose a plane shape or solid to reveal its key symmetry or structural properties.

Square: 4 symmetry lines; rotational order 4; smallest turn 90°.

4. Nets, views and cross-sections

A net is a connected flat pattern that folds without overlap into a solid. Top, front and side views are orthographic projections; a cross-section is the face exposed by a cut.

Cube net

Six connected squares are necessary, but not every arrangement of six squares folds into a cube.

Prism net

Two matching bases are joined by a strip of rectangular side faces.

Views

A cylinder standing upright has a circular top view and rectangular front view.

Cross-sections

A cut parallel to a prism's base has the same shape as that base.

Worked example: A solid has two triangular faces and three rectangular faces. Identify it and verify Euler's relation.
  1. The matching triangular bases and rectangular sides identify a triangular prism.
  2. It has F = 5, V = 6 and E = 9.
  3. F + V = 11 and E + 2 = 11, so Euler's relation is satisfied.
Visualisation habit: Mark which net edges will meet, and imagine rotating the solid rather than changing its structure.

🏅 Olympiad Corner: Symmetry & Solids

Use invariants: rotation preserves distances, folding pairs edges, and Euler's relation links face, edge and vertex counts.

Test folds or turns systematically. Count each edge or feature once. Track opposite faces in a net. Verify polyhedra with Euler's relation.

Example 1: Recover a missing solid count

A convex polyhedron has 8 faces and 12 vertices. How many edges does it have?

  1. Euler's relation is F + V = E + 2.
  2. Substitute: 8 + 12 = E + 2.
  3. Thus E = 20 − 2.
  4. The polyhedron has 18 edges.

Example 2: Painted cube pieces

A cube is painted on every face and cut into 27 equal small cubes. How many small cubes have exactly two painted faces?

  1. Exactly two painted faces occur on edge pieces that are not corners.
  2. Each large-cube edge has 3 small cubes, leaving 3 − 2 = 1 non-corner edge piece.
  3. A cube has 12 edges.
  4. Therefore 12 small cubes have exactly two painted faces.
